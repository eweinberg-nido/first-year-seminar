Overview / Unit 3

Unit 3 · Semester 2 · February – April

Harm Reduction & Substances

“Safety First” replaces scare tactics and “Just Say No” with honest, evidence-based, non-judgmental information. Abstinence is always presented as the safest choice. Students build a toolbox of ten harm-reduction strategies and apply it to every major drug class. They practise responding to emergencies and examine school drug policy. In parallel, a graphic-design strand in Canva builds toward a team harm-reduction campaign for their peers.

18 class days · Feb 17 – Apr 15 Product: Harm-reduction campaign (Instagram post, flyer, poster)

Driving question

  • How can harm reduction help us make decisions that manage our own risk and help our classmates?

Supporting questions

  • What is a drug, and how do values and culture shape what we believe about drugs?
  • Have scare tactics and “Just Say No” worked? Why or why not?
  • For each substance: what are the dangers, what are the signs of an emergency, and how should we respond?
  • What message is missing for teens, and how could we more effectively encourage teens to reduce drug-related harm?

Students will be able to…

  • Define a drug as “any substance that, in small amounts, produces changes in the brain, body, or both”, and analyze how values, culture and media shape beliefs about drug use.
  • Define harm reduction and apply the ten-strategy toolbox to themselves, to friends who use, and to future choices.
  • Categorize drug-related harms as Physical, Academic, Social-emotional or Legal, and recognize systemic disparities in legal consequences.
  • Place use on the continuum of substance use, from non-use to substance use disorder, without stigmatizing people who use drugs.
  • Explain how drugs act on neurons and neurotransmitters, and why the developing teenage brain is especially vulnerable.
  • For stimulants, nicotine, cannabis, depressants, opioids and psychedelics: describe effects, harms and benefits, and choose the strategies that fit.
  • Recognize an emergency for each drug class, place someone in the recovery position, and call 131 with the right information.
  • Evaluate health information with the CRAAP test and synthesize evidence from multiple sources.
  • Analyze zero-tolerance and school drug policy, including Nido’s handbook, and know where to find support.
  • Design a message for a specific teen audience using color, typography, layout and responsibly sourced images.

Approach

How harm reduction is framed for students, and the norms that keep the conversation safe.

We do not…

  • Judge teens who use drugs
  • Encourage or condone drug use
  • Teach how to use drugs

We do…

  • Treat abstinence as the primary strategy
  • Acknowledge that some teens will try drugs
  • Empower students with accurate information
  • Give concrete steps to reduce harm

The core message throughout is: “It’s always safest not to use alcohol or other drugs.” The toolbox is for three situations: a student who is using, a student whose friends are using, and a student who abstains now but may face a choice later.

The harm-reduction toolbox

  • Abstinence
  • Develop drug knowledge
  • Dose & dosage
  • Start low, go slow
  • Set & setting
  • Check the substance
  • Know how to respond to an emergency
  • Know drug policy
  • Consider moderation
  • Don’t mix substances

The psychedelics lesson also adds having a sober sitter.

Classroom norms

One Mic

One speaker at a time, so every voice is heard.

Step Up / Step Back

Students who speak often make room; quieter students step in.

Good intent, real impact

Don’t assume malice; name the impact (“What you said made me feel…”) or ask a clarifying question.

Vegas Rule

What is shared in the room stays in the room.

S.W.I.M.

“Someone Who Isn’t Me”: “I know someone who…”, never names or personal details.

Ideas, not people

Disagree with ideas, not people. Teachers revisit the norms whenever opinions clash.

Daily routines

Mindful Moment (3 min)

Box breathing, 7/11 breathing, finger breathing or meditation to open class.

Do Now (5 min)

A prior-knowledge prompt or scenario, shared before the lesson objectives are read aloud.

Movement & debate

Philosophical Chairs, agree/disagree corners, sticker gallery walks and re-votes after research.

Anonymous exit tickets

Students write 2–3 questions; the teacher answers them at the start of a later class.

What students asked on Day 1

Their exit-ticket questions are reviewed on Day 3 and shape the unit:

  • How do I know if someone is overdosing, and how can I help?
  • How do different drugs work, and why do some make you feel happy?
  • What are the physical and psychological harms?
  • Why do people use drugs, beyond peer pressure?
  • Are some people more likely to get addicted, including to prescription drugs? How do you stop?
  • How do minors get drugs, and how do I handle peer pressure?

Day by day

The 2026 sequence as taught. Health lessons and design lessons run side by side. Open any day for the lesson detail.

Foundations · Days 1 – 3

  1. Day 1Tue Feb 17

    Lesson 1 · What is a drug?

    Reconnections walk, an anonymous pre-survey, the Safety First philosophy, classroom norms, and debating beliefs about drugs.

    Lesson detail
    • Do Now on sticky notes: “What is a drug?” and “What comes to mind when you hear the word?”
    • Anonymous, ungraded pre-survey to set a baseline (15 min).
    • Scare tactics and “Just Say No” vs. evidence-based approaches: “Do you think these methods have worked?”
    • Abstinence vs. harm reduction, using smoking as the example.
    • “Is it a drug?” — eight items checked against the definition. Answer: they are all drugs.
    • Philosophical Chairs on ten statements, e.g. “People who use drugs are bad” and “Drug addiction is an illness”.
    • Exit ticket: what do 9th graders need to know about drugs?
  2. Day 2Thu Feb 19

    Lesson 2 · Introduction to harm reduction

    Defining harm reduction, perception vs. data, why teens use, the four kinds of harm, and the continuum of use. The campaign project is launched.

    Lesson detail
    • Do Now: spot harm reduction in everyday images (seatbelts, healthy food, protective gear), then write a definition.
    • “Which drug do teens use most?” — prediction, then NIDA data: alcohol, cannabis, tobacco, prescription drugs.
    • Trends since the 1990s (Monitoring the Future): many teens choose not to use, which counters “everyone is doing it”.
    • Why teens use: curiosity, feeling better, fitting in, performance, fun.
    • Predict-and-compare sort of harms into Physical, Academic, Social-emotional and Legal, including systemic disparities in legal harms.
    • Continuum of use: six case studies mapped from non-use to misuse; the “Alex” case introduces substance use disorder and stigma.
    • Creative Communicator launch: the PSA brief (due Apr 15) and Canva onboarding.
    • Creative Communicator L2: Canva Instagram template
  3. Day 3Mon Feb 24

    Lesson 3 · How drugs work & the harm-reduction toolbox

    Neurons, neurotransmitters and the teenage brain, then each of the ten strategies in turn.

    Lesson detail
    • Students’ exit-ticket questions reviewed.
    • Brain, neurons and synapses; dopamine, acetylcholine, GABA and serotonin; use vs. physical dependence vs. addiction.
    • Video “The Workings of the Adolescent Mind”. 1 in 7 people who start drinking by 14 develop problems, compared with 1 in 50 who wait until 21. Writing: why delay use?
    • Harm reduction in action: Matt (water between drinks), Lisa (cannabis before a test), Maria (coffee to sober up — a trick question).
    • The toolbox, strategy by strategy: drug checking kits, “crossfading”, Chilean emergency numbers, knowing school policy.

Stimulants & nicotine · Days 4 – 7

  1. Day 4

    Party scenarios · Lesson 4 · Stimulants & caffeine

    Planning for safety (Indira and Nick), how stimulants work, and “Caffeine Madness”.

    Lesson detail
    • Indira: what to think about before a party — refusal, mood, food and sleep, limits, a trusted friend, a safe ride home.
    • Nick: legal status, strangers, an isolated setting near water. Never use alone.
    • Fatima’s late-night study scenario introduces stimulants (“uppers”): effects, risks and dependence.
    • Caffeine: how it blocks adenosine, a half-life of about 6 hours, and a bracket game ranking the caffeine in everyday products.
    • Stimulant strategies, including keeping caffeine under 150 mg a day.
    • Communicators Lesson 2: color & fonts
  2. Day 5

    Layout & design · Stimulants mini-research

    Design principles applied to a redesign, then research on prescription and illicit stimulants.

    Lesson detail
    • Layout: relative size, white space, grounding, flow, alignment. “If everything is important, nothing is.”
    • Design task part 2: rebuild an earlier design, then submit three images with reflections.
    • Mini-research on cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine and methylphenidate: harms, reasons for use, one strategy.
    • Over-amping, cocaethylene (cocaine with alcohol), and prescription vs. street supply. Only take what is prescribed to you.
    • Creative Communicator L2: three Canva images
    • Knowledge Constructor L2: stimulants research
  3. Day 6

    Nicotine & vaping · CRAAP research

    Nicotine and the brain, inside an e-cigarette, aerosols vs. “water vapor”, then researching whether vaping is a healthier alternative.

    Lesson detail
    • Do Now: what do you know and want to know about vapes, pouches and e-cigarettes?
    • A short history of vaping; students audit vape ads in their own social media feeds. Marketing nicotine to minors is prohibited in Chile.
    • Video “Smoking vs. Vaping”: is vaping a healthier alternative?
    • The CRAAP test, then four sources (two for, two against) and a synthesis paragraph with at least one quote.
    • L3/L2 vaping synthesis paragraph
  4. Day 7

    Marketing & misinformation · Nicotine flyer

    How advertising and groups with an agenda shape information, how design changes a message, and the L3 flyer.

    Lesson detail
    • Warm-up: vape brands, ads and social media, and their role in teen decisions.
    • Who has an agenda in information about cannabis, and why inaccurate drug information is dangerous.
    • Analyzing “think before you act”: alignment, repetition, color and font.
    • L3 task: an A4 flyer on vaping, smoking or pouches using one strategy, text and color only. Note that “start low, go slow” doesn’t work for nicotine.
    • Creative Communicator L3: nicotine flyer

Cannabis, alcohol, opioids & psychedelics · Days 8 – 11

  1. Day 8

    Cannabis: fact or fiction

    Sticker votes on seven statements, CRAAP modelled on two sources, group research, then the science of edibles and a re-vote.

    Lesson detail
    • Green/red/yellow stickers on statements such as “Cannabis is safe because it is natural” and “Edible cannabis is safer than smoked cannabis”.
    • The teacher models CRAAP on a Medical Daily article and a Reddit thread: does cannabis make you hungry?
    • Groups of 2–4 research one statement with four sources, then share their verdict and strongest evidence.
    • THC and CBD; smoked vs. edible (video “Your Brain on Edible Marijuana”).
    • “Eddie and the edibles”: edibles take 45–90 minutes to kick in, so wait at least 2 hours before more.
    • Chilean law (decriminalized for personal use) and SENDA 2023 data; adult legality is not youth permission.
  2. Day 9

    Alcohol & depressants · Emergency response

    Myths vs. facts, the standard-drink pour, drink spiking, and recognizing and responding to alcohol poisoning.

    Lesson detail
    • Prediction: what percentage of high schoolers drink? Then a true/false myth Do Now (coffee, food, “sleeping it off”).
    • Standard-drink activity: students pour beer, wine and spirit estimates into party cups, then measure.
    • Roofies and eight habits to prevent drink spiking. Never mix depressants (e.g. benzos and alcohol).
    • C.U.P.S., Good Samaritan laws (“Does Chile have one?”), recovery-position practice in pairs, and rehearsing a 131 call.
    • “I drank too much and…” empathy activity on binge drinking and blackouts. Homework: Teen Talk worksheet.
    • Functional Health Knowledge L2: binge drinking
  3. Day 10

    Lesson 9 · Opioids · Intellectual property

    Prescription and illicit opioids, fentanyl, the overdose crisis, naloxone, and copyright vs. fair use vs. Creative Commons.

    Lesson detail
    • History, mechanism, tolerance and addiction; video “Your Brain on Fentanyl”.
    • The overdose crisis, from prescriptions to heroin to fentanyl, and its effect on families and friends.
    • A friend wants to try opioids: what would you tell them? Does the advice change for prescription opioids vs. heroin?
    • Drug-checking demonstration with two pitchers; why a “pass” doesn’t mean safe; speedballing.
    • Video “What Is An Opioid Overdose?”, naloxone, and the Tony exit ticket.
    • Homework: a news article on the opioid crisis compared with the 1980s crack epidemic, including the article’s tone.
    • Copyright, fair use and Creative Commons through an Etsy poster-shop scenario.
    • Functional Health Knowledge L2: opioids & emergency response
  4. Day 11

    Lesson 10 · Psychedelics

    Drugs in music, LSD, psilocybin, MDMA and Tusi, current research, risks, and emergency signs.

    Lesson detail
    • Does music glorify or judge drug use? Teens hear an estimated 84 drug references in songs every day.
    • Ten minutes of independent research on one psychedelic: form, effects, duration, peak.
    • Tusi: originally 2C-B, now an unpredictable mix of ketamine, caffeine and MDMA in Latin America.
    • Research on psychedelic-assisted therapy; video “Facts About MDMA” with See–Think–Wonder.
    • Set and setting, a sober sitter, starting low, and MDMA hydration (about one 8 oz bottle per hour, not more).

Emergencies, policy & the campaign · Days 12 – 18

  1. Day 12

    Emergency scenarios assessment

    Partner role-play: one student plays a person in distress without naming the drug. The responder identifies the substance, assesses symptoms, gives care, and briefs EMS (played by the teacher).

    • Substances L4 · Collaboration L2
  2. Day 13

    Drug policy & campaign launch

    Zero tolerance vs. harm reduction, Nido’s handbook, support and counseling, how anti-drug messages succeed or fail, and the campaign brief.

    Lesson detail
    • Review: which strategies matter most for each substance, and why “start low, go slow” fails for nicotine.
    • Agree/disagree on four statements, e.g. “Harsh punishments ensure abstinence among teens”.
    • Nido handbook: “major” vs. “intolerable” offenses and their procedures; how to access counseling; video “Drugs & coping”.
    • Critiquing a school policy message (“Does it change anyone’s behavior?”), anti-drug ads, and why DARE failed.
    • Message, medium and audience; strengths and limits of images, text, infographics and posters.
    • Campaign brief: teams of three, five strategies to choose from, three pieces in one consistent style.
  3. Days 14 – 16to Tue Apr 7

    Campaign planning & research

    Campaign planner, a two-minute rapid brainstorm, visual metaphors, choosing an audience and substance, CRAAP-checked information, and peer feedback on page 1.

  4. Day 17Thu Apr 9

    A Campaign for a Healthy Future

    Do Now: “What is the harm you are trying to reduce? How are you communicating it?” Students find Creative Commons images and work toward the Collaboration 3.0 target.

  5. Day 18Mon Apr 13

    Peer feedback & submission

    Three-part peer review (audience, what works, what doesn’t), revisions, then submission: individual poster to Schoology, group campaign to the shared slide deck. Due Wed Apr 15.

Harm reduction by substance

The strategies each lesson emphasizes, and the key facts students learn.

SubstanceStrategies emphasizedKey facts students learn
Caffeine & stimulantsStart low, go slow · Don’t mix · Dose & dosage · Only take what’s prescribed to you · Use a weaker stimulant · Know drug policyKeep caffeine under 150 mg a day. Over-amping is the stimulant equivalent of an overdose. Cocaine with alcohol forms cocaethylene. Crushing or snorting pills raises the risk of addiction and overdose.
Nicotine & vapingAbstinence · Develop drug knowledgeNicotine is highly addictive, especially for teens; aerosols are not “water vapor”. Start low, go slow does not work for nicotine, because even low doses cause addiction.
CannabisAbstinence (delay to at least 18) · Start low, go slow · Develop drug knowledge · Know drug policyEdibles take 45–90 minutes to kick in; wait at least 2 hours before more. Synthetic cannabinoids such as K2 can be deadly. Decriminalization for adults is not permission for youth.
Alcohol & depressantsAbstinence · Dose & dosage · Don’t mix · Know how to respond to an emergencyStandard drink sizes; coffee doesn’t sober you up. Benzos with alcohol sharply raise overdose risk. Learn C.U.P.S. and the eight habits that prevent drink spiking.
OpioidsCheck the substance · Start low, go slow · Dose & dosage · Know how to respond to an emergencyIllicit supply is often laced with fentanyl. Mixing with depressants or stimulants is especially dangerous. Naloxone reverses an opioid overdose. Never share prescriptions.
Psychedelics, MDMA & TusiSet & setting · Sober sitter · Start low, go slow · Check the substanceEffects are unpredictable, with higher risk for people with a family history of mental illness. MDMA can cause overheating, so sip about 8 oz of water an hour, not more. Tusi’s contents vary.

Emergency response

Taught with every drug class and assessed on Day 12. Students write these in their study guide.

Alcohol poisoning: C.U.P.S.

Cold, clammy skin · Unconsciousness · Puking while passed out · Slowed or irregular breathing (normal for 13–17 is 12–16 breaths a minute).

Opioid overdose

Slow, shallow or no breathing; pinpoint pupils; snoring or gurgling; unresponsive and limp; blue or purple lips. Naloxone reverses it.

MDMA & Tusi

Faintness, overheating, inability to sweat, seizures, loss of consciousness. With Tusi, also consider fentanyl.

LSD & mushrooms

Difficulty breathing or walking, high heart rate, seizure, very agitated states.

Respond

1. Call 131: nothing should stop you calling. 2. Put the person in the recovery position. 3. Tell the dispatcher who you are, where you are, who needs help, their symptoms, and what happened before.

Ambulance (SAMU) 131 · Firefighters 132 · Carabineros 133 · Clínica Alemana +56 2 2910 9911 · Clínica Las Condes +56 2 2610 7777

Design strand

The Creative Communicator lessons that run alongside the health content and build to the campaign.

  1. Canva onboarding Day 2

    Sign in with a school account and start from an Instagram template. No AI design tools: version history shows each student’s own design process.

  2. Color & fonts Day 4

    Palettes and HEX codes, readability and contrast. One sentence designed three ways, sad, silly and professional, using only two colors and fonts.

  3. Layout, flow & alignment Day 5

    Size, white space, grounding and eye path. Students rebuild an earlier design and reflect on what changed.

  4. Design analysis & nicotine flyer Day 7

    Deconstructing “think before you act”, then an A4 flyer from a blank canvas using text and color only (L3).

  5. Intellectual property Day 10

    Copyright, fair use and Creative Commons.

  6. Message, medium & audience Day 13

    Analyzing past campaigns and the strengths of different formats.

  7. The campaign Days 14 – 18

    Three pieces in one consistent style, Creative Commons images, and at most one AI-generated image per poster.
